Physical AI: Intelligence Leaves the Screen

For a decade, AI lived in data centers and apps. Now the same models that learned to read and write are learning to see, grasp and move. The largest markets for intelligence may turn out to be physical ones.

Mahayana ResearchOctober 2026 · 10 min read
IN THIS PERSPECTIVE
  1. Why now
  2. The physical AI stack
  3. What remains hard
  4. Questions for founders

Robotics has promised a revolution for half a century and delivered it only in narrow, carefully engineered settings. Each new task required new code, new fixtures and months of tuning. Robots were precise, but they could not generalize.

That constraint is beginning to lift. Models trained on vast amounts of video, simulation and real-world interaction can now transfer skills across tasks and environments. A robot no longer needs to be programmed for every motion; it can be taught, corrected and improved—much like software.

01

Why this time is different

Three curves are converging. Foundation models give machines a general understanding of objects, language and intent. Simulation and synthetic data make it possible to practice millions of scenarios before touching the real world. And the cost of actuators, sensors and edge compute keeps falling, driven by electric vehicles and consumer electronics supply chains.

When robots can learn instead of being programmed, every physical workflow becomes a software opportunity.

Demand is equally structural. Aging populations, labor shortages in manufacturing and logistics, and the reshoring of supply chains are creating persistent pressure to automate work that has resisted it for decades.

02

The physical AI stack

01

Robot foundation models

General policies that map perception and instruction to action, improving with every hour of deployment data.

02

Data and simulation

Teleoperation, digital twins and synthetic environments that generate the training signal the physical world rarely provides for free.

03

Hardware platforms

Arms, mobile bases and humanoid forms designed for cost, safety and manufacturability—not just for demos.

04

Vertical deployments

Companies that own a specific workflow—picking, assembly, inspection, care—and deliver measurable productivity to customers.

03

What remains genuinely hard

The physical world is unforgiving. Reliability that is acceptable in a chatbot is unacceptable in a factory: a robot that succeeds ninety-five times out of a hundred may still be unusable. Safety certification, maintenance, integration with existing equipment and unit economics all decide whether a pilot becomes a fleet.

This is why we pay close attention to deployment. The winners will not be the teams with the most impressive videos, but those that turn early customers into a data flywheel—each installed robot making the next one more capable and less expensive to support.

In physical AI, the demo is the beginning. Uptime is the business.
04

Five questions we ask founders

  1. 01Which task do you perform reliably today, and what does reliability mean to the customer?
  2. 02How does every deployed unit improve your models and your economics?
  3. 03What is your path from pilot to fleet, and who pays for integration?
  4. 04Where do you build hardware yourselves, and where do you rely on the supply chain?
  5. 05How do you prove safety to customers and regulators?

The next interface for AI has hands.

The first era of AI changed how we work with information. The next will change how we make, move and care for things. We are looking for founders who combine frontier models with the discipline of industrial engineering—and the patience to earn trust on the factory floor.
