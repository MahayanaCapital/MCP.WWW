Software That Does the Work: The Outcome Economy

For forty years, software helped people do their jobs. AI agents are beginning to do the job itself. That shift moves value from seats to outcomes—and opens markets far larger than software has ever addressed.

Mahayana ResearchOctober 2026 · 10 min read
IN THIS PERSPECTIVE
  1. From tools to work
  2. The services opportunity
  3. What winners build
  4. Questions for founders

Every software era has been defined by what it sold. Licenses sold ownership. SaaS sold access, priced by the seat. The emerging era sells something different: the finished piece of work.

When an AI agent can reconcile the ledger, draft the contract, triage the ticket or prepare the claim, the customer no longer buys a tool for an employee. It buys the result. This is not a pricing tweak. It is a change in what software is.

01

From tools people use to systems that deliver

Traditional software sat beside the worker. Its value was capped by the number of people who logged in and the hours they spent there. Agents remove that ceiling. Their value scales with the volume of work they complete, and their cost falls with every model generation.

The market for software was always the IT budget. The market for outcomes is the labor budget.

That distinction matters because labor spend across law, accounting, healthcare administration, insurance and customer operations dwarfs what those same industries have ever spent on software. The companies that convert even a fraction of that work into reliable, automated outcomes can build businesses with a different scale and shape.

02

Services are the new software market

The most interesting opportunities are not in horizontal assistants, but in specific, high-volume workflows where outcomes are measurable and errors are costly. These are areas long served by outsourcing firms and professional services—where customers already pay for results.

01

Measurable outcomes

The work has a clear definition of done: a claim filed, a reconciliation closed, a document reviewed. Pricing can follow results.

02

High volume, repeatable

Thousands of similar tasks create the feedback needed to improve quality and lower cost with every cycle.

03

Expensive errors

Where mistakes matter, trust becomes the product. Companies that earn it through accuracy and auditability are hard to displace.

04

Fragmented incumbents

Markets served by many small providers are open to a new category leader that delivers better quality at software margins.

03

What the winners will build

Model capability is necessary but not sufficient. Enduring outcome companies combine three layers: deep domain judgment encoded into the workflow, a human-in-the-loop design that catches the hard cases, and an operating model that turns every exception into training signal.

They also rethink go-to-market. Selling outcomes means owning accountability. The best founders design contracts, service levels and reporting around the result—making it easy for a buyer to compare them not with other software, but with the cost and quality of the people or providers they replace.

The moat is not the agent. It is the reliability of the outcome, compounded over millions of tasks.
04

Five questions we ask founders

  1. 01What is the unit of work you deliver, and how does the customer know it is done well?
  2. 02Which budget do you replace—software, outsourcing or headcount?
  3. 03How do your margins change as models improve and volumes grow?
  4. 04Where do humans stay in the loop, and how does that shrink over time?
  5. 05What do you learn from each exception that a competitor cannot?

Sell the work, not the tool.

The seat-based era rewarded software that people wanted to use. The outcome era will reward systems that customers can rely on. We are looking for founders who see entire service industries as software markets waiting to be rebuilt.
